# Appendix. Handling the ship

## What this appendix is for

A rig is built to be worked. Knowing how the crew handles the sails explains why the ropes go where they go
(Chapters 06–08), and it gives a game its most honest mechanics. This is a short summary; the full commands are in
Lever [1].

Confidence labels as in [Chapter 01](01-ship-type-and-era.md): (period; here Lever 1808 and Steel 1794), (secondary),
(our inference). The numbers of men and the times are **our estimates (our inference)** unless a source is given; we
found no logbook data for them.

## Basic operations

**Setting a square sail (our inference, from [1]):** men aloft cast off the gaskets and let the sail fall; on deck the
clewlines and buntlines are eased while the sheets are hauled home; for a topsail the yard is then hoisted with the
halliards and the yards are braced to the wind; the lifts are eased so the yard hangs on its tie.

**Furling a topsail** [1] (period): first the weather sheet, then the bowline and the lee sheet; the sail aback, the
bunt hauled up, the sail furled on the yard.

**Reefing a topsail** [1] (period): lower the halliard (the yard comes down), haul up the clewlines, brace in the
weather brace to spill the wind from the sail, haul out the reef tackles; men on the yard; the weather earing first,
then the lee earing, then the reef points.

**Taking in the jib** [1] (period): man the downhaul, let go the halliard, ease the sheet, haul the sail down the stay,
pull it in under the bowsprit cap and stow it.

**Bracing** [2] (period), (our inference): the braces of one side are hauled while those of the other side are eased;
the yards of a mast are braced together. Braced sharp up the yards turn about 30–35° (our inference); the alternated
shroud pairs exist so the yards can be braced sharper [2] (period). See Chapter 07 for the limits in the reference
model.

## Manoeuvres

### Tacking (going about head to wind)

Sources: [1] (period); [3], [4] (secondary).

1. Prepare: weather braces, lee tacks, weather sheets, lee bowlines manned. "Ready about!"
2. Helm down: "Helm's a-lee!"; ease the fore sheet, the fore topsail bowline and the sheets of the jib and staysails.
   Lever advises easing the helm over gradually, not putting it hard over at once.
3. When the wind is about 3 points (33°) on the bow: "Off tacks and sheets!"; ease the main tack and sheet.
4. Almost head to wind: "Mainsail haul!"; swing the main yards.
5. When the main sails fill: "Let go and haul!"; swing the fore yards.
6. Braces sharp up, bowlines hauled, ropes cleared.

With a small crew the yards are braced first and the head sails shifted after [3] (secondary). **Missed stays:** haul
the main yard aback until the ship gathers sternway, then haul the fore sheet and pay off; a well-manned ship can
box-haul instead [1] (period).

### Wearing (turning stern through the wind)

Source: [1] (period).

Man the main clewlines, ease the main tack and bowline, haul up the weather clew, then the lee one; helm up; as the
ship falls off ease the fore bowline and sheet and keep the braces working; with the wind aft, board the main tack on
the new side and haul the sheet; brace sharp up as she comes to. The ship turns through about 240° and loses ground
to leeward [5] (secondary). Used in very light winds with a swell (below about 2 knots) or in strong winds [3]
(secondary).

### Heaving to

Source: [1] (period).

One topsail (main or fore) aback, the rest full: the ship almost stops. In a gale: heave to under the mizen staysail
alone (on a ship; a brig or snow has no mizen, so the main staysail), under a close-reefed main topsail, or under the
fore course.

### Anchoring and weighing

Source: [1] (period).

**Anchoring:** take in the topgallants and courses, stream the anchor buoy, clew up the topsails, let go when the ship
comes head to wind and loses way; in a tideway head into the stream; when anchoring in a tideway, topsails on three
reefs. **Weighing:** heave the cable short (to the stay-peak), stopper it, sheet home the three topsails, heave the
anchor up, then "Helm's a-lee" and "Mainsail haul" when the wind is about one point on the weather bow.

### Emergencies

Source: [1] (period).

- **Club-hauling** (on a lee shore when tacking fails): let go the lee anchor, the bow comes to the wind, cut the cable,
  the ship pays off on the other tack.
- **Box-hauling:** helm a-lee, fore yards braced aback, the ship turns almost in her own length.
- **Scudding** (running before a gale): close-reefed main topsail and fore course, fore topmast staysail ready; danger
  to the rudder if she gathers sternway.
- **Striking the topmasts** in a storm: less windage, better stability; cutting away masts only as a last resort to
  save the ship.

## Damage and repairs

- **Shroud or stay shot through:** knot it (shroud knot) or splice it and set it up with the lanyard; before battle
  preventer braces are rove and the lower yards slung with chains [1], [2] (period).
- **Sprung mast or yard:** fish it with pieces of a spare spar and woldings (rope bands) [1], [2] (period).
- **Lost topmast:** clear the wreck, send up the spare topmast with the top-rope through the block at the cap, drive
  the fid, rig shrouds and backstays again in the order of Chapter 06 [2] (period).
- **Jury mast** from spare spars or yards [1] (period).
- **New ropes stretch:** after a gale the shrouds must be set up with the lanyards [2] (period).

## Game mechanics that follow from real handling (our inference)

1. **Tacking as a rhythm of commands** [1]: the player gives "Helm's a-lee", "Mainsail haul" and "Let go and haul"
   inside windows of the bow's angle to the wind (e.g. ±10° around head to wind). Too late: missed stays and sternway,
   then a choice between hauling the main aback, wearing, or club-hauling with an anchor.
2. **Bracing with three sliders** (fore yards, main yards, topsails separately), with limits that come from the shrouds
   and backstays [2]. Badly braced main topsails take the wind from the fore sails.
3. **Crew assigned to stations** (braces, halliards, men aloft, helm) [3]: a small crew must work step by step, so a
   tack takes longer; a natural reason to recruit.
4. **The reefing decision** [1]: reef the topsails before a squall (time, men aloft, risk of a fall) or risk the
   topmast. The number of reefs depends on the period: 1–2 in 1710.
5. **Heaving to with a topsail aback** as the mode for boarding, taking a boat aboard or sounding [1].
6. **Anchoring and weighing with the tide** [1]: wind and current decide how to anchor; a poor manoeuvre ends with a
   dragging anchor. Club-hauling as a last resort on a lee shore.
7. **A load model for the rigging** [2], [5]: every shroud, stay and backstay has a state; a hit or an overload parts
   it; a topmast without its stay may break when close-hauled. The rigging order of Chapter 06 as a repair minigame.
8. **Preparing for battle** [1], [2]: chains on the slings, preventer braces, nettings. Costs time and men, lowers the
   risk of a yard coming down.
9. **Striking topmasts in a storm** [1]: a trade between speed and safety and stability.
10. **Rope maintenance** [1], [2]: tar, spun-yarn and canvas as resources; worming, parcelling and serving as a short
    minigame; neglected shrouds rot and part.
11. **Setting up the shrouds after a gale** [2]: new hemp stretches; slack shrouds mean a higher risk of losing a mast
    and worse bracing angles.
12. **A clear deck as reputation in port** (our inference): furled sails and coils on the pins raise reputation; a mess
    of ropes slows the next manoeuvre (fouled lines).
